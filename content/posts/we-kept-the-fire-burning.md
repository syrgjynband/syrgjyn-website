*Six years. Two musicians. One drum machine. Hundreds of gigs. And a fire that refused to die.*

There are probably easier ways to spend six years of your life than playing extreme metal with a drum machine in a small underground club in Tirana, Albania.

We just couldn't think of any good reasons to choose them.

From September 2005 until August 2011, every Saturday night, SYRGJYN took the stage at Alkimia. And by "the band," we mean two human beings and a drum machine, armed with whatever equipment we could afford, borrow, repair, or somehow convince to work.

No spectacular stage production. No professional sound crew. No fancy equipment. Sometimes, barely enough gear to call it a proper live setup.

Just two stubborn airheads who loved music too much to stop playing it.

And play we did.

For six years.

Every. Fucking. Saturday.

## A Drum Machine Walks Into a Bar...

To understand what those nights were like, you have to forget most of what you've come to expect from a live metal performance.

There was no drummer hiding behind a mountain of cymbals. No impressive wall of amplifiers. No elaborate light show.

There were two guys, a drum machine, and a rather ambitious idea of what a Saturday night should sound like.

The idea was simple: **play everything we loved, play it loud, and give the people a show worth coming back for.**

Our sets lasted three hours and a half up to four hours. One 10 minute bathroom break in the middle. 

Not forty minutes. Not a carefully selected handful of songs designed to promote the latest release.

Three to four hours of rock, heavy metal, punk, thrash, gothic metal, death metal, black metal, and everything we could squeeze between them.

One moment you could hear AC/DC, Stones, Purple, Scorpions, or KISS. Then we'd move into Judas Priest or Iron Maiden.

Type O Negative and Tiamat would drag the atmosphere into darker territory. The Ramones would kick the doors back open.

Then came Metallica, Megadeth, Slayer, Sodom and Sepultura.

And if things weren't sufficiently brutal already, there was always Deicide, Morbid Angel, Death, Hypocrisy, Dimmu Borgir, Burzum, Darkthrone, or Impaled Nazarene.

Somewhere in that glorious musical disorder, we'd throw in our own SYRGJYN material.

Imagine walking into a club expecting a few familiar rock songs and finding yourself, a couple of hours later, in the middle of a black metal set.

There wasn't much room for boredom.

Or subtlety.

We weren't trying to please everyone. We were sharing the music that had shaped us, from the anthems that introduced generations to rock to the darkest corners of the underground.

And we wanted to play all of it.

## Six Years Is a Long Time to Be Stubborn

There is something peculiar about repetition.

Do something once, and it's an event.

Do it for a few months, and it becomes a habit.

Do it every Saturday for six years, and it becomes part of who you are.

That's what Alkimia became for us.

Week after week, year after year, we showed up and played.

Across that period, hundreds of Saturday nights turned into an enormous amount of time spent performing, preparing, rehearsing, learning songs, fixing equipment, and doing everything necessary to make the next gig happen.

The repertoire alone was a considerable undertaking.

Think about the musical ground covered between Angus Young and Trey Azagthoth, between Joey Ramone and Fenriz, between the theatrical darkness of Type O Negative and the relentless assault of Slayer.

Different tunings played on one tuning. Different techniques. Different vocal approaches. Completely different musical worlds.

We didn't have the luxury of separate bands specializing in separate genres.

We wanted to play the music, so we figured out how to play the music.

It wasn't always pretty.

But it was real.

And somewhere along the way, without necessarily realizing it, we were receiving an education that no music school could have offered us.

**You cannot spend that many nights onstage without learning something about music, people, failure, endurance, and yourself.**

You learn how to keep going when your equipment isn't cooperating.

You learn that enthusiasm can carry a performance surprisingly far.

You learn that sometimes the audience doesn't care about half the things musicians obsess over.

And you learn that the difference between someone who dreams of being in a band and someone who actually plays in one is often embarrassingly simple.

One of them gets onstage.

## The People on the Other Side of the Noise

Over those six years, countless faces passed through “Alkimia”.

Some came because they loved metal. Others were curious. Some were regulars. Some probably had no idea what they were getting themselves into.

There were people who knew every word, people discovering entire genres for the first time, and people who simply wanted somewhere to belong.

Because that's something outsiders often misunderstand about underground music.

It's never just about the music.

For some people, a small club with loud guitars is the only place where the world makes sense.

It's where you meet people who understand why a particular riff matters. Where nobody thinks your black T-shirt collection is a psychological problem. Where you can spend an entire evening arguing about which Metallica album is the best and somehow consider it time well spent.

But beneath the leather, the long hair, the beer, and the noise, something else was happening.

People were watching.

Not just listening to the songs, but watching what was possible.

They saw two musicians performing with equipment that was, to put it diplomatically, nowhere near professional standards.

They saw a band without a human drummer.

They saw mistakes, limitations, and all the imperfections that come with doing something yourself.

And they saw us doing it anyway.

That last part turned out to matter more than we could have imagined.

## You Don't Need Permission to Rock 'n' Roll

Over the years, we discovered that some of the people who had spent their Saturday nights watching SYRGJYN eventually started bands of their own.

Think about that for a moment.

Not because we had shown them what a perfect band should look like.

Quite the opposite.

We had shown them that you could be imperfect, under-equipped, understaffed, and still make something happen.

You could have two musicians instead of five.

You could have a drum machine instead of a drummer.

You could have an amplifier that had seen better decades.

You could have almost nothing resembling the ideal conditions for a live performance.

And you could still get up there and play your heart out.

Perhaps that was the most useful thing we ever demonstrated.

Not some extraordinary musical technique. Not some secret formula for writing a great song.

Just the simple, liberating realization that **you don't have to wait until everything is right before you begin.**

If you love something enough, you find a way.

And if there isn't a way, you make one.

We didn't invent that philosophy. Rock 'n' roll had been teaching it long before any of us were born.

But for a few years, in our little corner of Tirana, we had the privilege of putting it into practice every single week.

And some people took that lesson home.

They picked up instruments. They found bandmates. They started writing songs. They began creating their own noise, their own identities, their own stories.

That's a remarkable thing to witness.

Because influence isn't always about inspiring people to become like you.

Sometimes, **the greatest influence you can have is convincing someone that they can become themselves.**

## Keeping the Underground Alive

Scenes don't survive on enthusiasm alone.

They need people who organize concerts, open venues, lend equipment, distribute music, show up at gigs, buy records, support bands, and keep participating even when almost nobody is paying attention.

They need musicians who are willing to play for an audience of twenty with the same commitment they'd give to an audience of twenty thousand.

They need stubbornness.

And they need continuity.

The history of Albanian underground metal is full of people who contributed in different ways, often with limited resources and little recognition. Bands formed, musicians came and went, venues opened and closed, and entire periods passed with very few opportunities to perform.

We have enormous respect for everyone who carried a piece of that history.

But we also know what those six years meant to us and to the people who were there.

There were times when the underground felt dangerously close to silence.

Times when opportunities were scarce, conditions were miserable, and maintaining any kind of regular live activity required an almost irrational level of determination.

We kept playing.

Not because somebody had appointed us guardians of the scene.

Not because we believed we were more important than anyone else.

And certainly not because there was some glorious financial reward waiting at the end of another three-hour Saturday night.

We played because we loved it.

Because it was part of our lives.

Because every time we plugged in, there was a room full of reasons to keep going.

Looking back, we feel an enormous sense of pride in the role SYRGJYN played during those years.

But even more than pride, we feel gratitude.

**We had the privilege of holding the torch at a time when keeping it lit wasn't always easy.**

And the beautiful thing about a torch is that its purpose isn't to be the only source of light.

It's to light others.

## The Fire Doesn't Belong to Anyone

There's a temptation, when you've been around long enough, to start treating history like private property.

To count your gigs, list your achievements, remind everybody how long you've been there, and demand recognition for every contribution.

We've been doing this since 1999. We have our scars, our stories, our recordings, and more hours of live experience than we can reasonably count.

We're proud of all of it.

But we don't believe the value of those years can be measured only by what SYRGJYN achieved.

A much more interesting question is what happened around us.

How many friendships began at those gigs?

How many people discovered a band they'd never heard before?

How many musicians took their first steps because they realized they didn't need expensive equipment or perfect conditions?

How many future concerts, songs, recordings, and bands can trace some small part of their existence back to a Saturday night at Alkimia?

We'll probably never know.

And that's perfectly fine.

Because the moment a fire spreads, it stops being about the person holding the original torch.

It becomes something bigger.

Something shared.

Something that can outlive the venue, the equipment, the musicians, and even the memories of those particular nights.

That's what an underground scene is supposed to be.

Not a monument to the people who came first.

**A place where the people who come next can find the courage to begin.**

## Go With What You Got

Today, one of the principles of the SYRGJYN Codex is:

**Go with what you got. Even if all you got is balls.**

It might sound like the kind of thing you'd find written on a backstage wall after too many beers.

But it comes from somewhere very real.

From years of showing up with equipment that wasn't good enough, resources that weren't enough, and circumstances that were rarely ideal.

From learning that waiting for perfect conditions is one of the easiest ways to spend your entire life doing absolutely nothing.

From discovering that limitations don't always destroy creativity.

Sometimes they give it a reason to exist.

We could have waited for a proper drummer.

We could have waited for better equipment.

We could have waited for a larger audience, a better venue, more money, more support, or a more promising future for metal in Albania.

Had we done that, hundreds of gigs would never have happened.

Thousands of songs would never have been played.

People who came through those doors might never have witnessed what we were doing.

And some of the stories that followed might have turned out differently.

Instead, we plugged in.

And played.

Again.

And again.

And again.

## To Everyone Who Was There

Alkimia was never just two musicians and a drum machine.

It was also the people who came back every Saturday.

The ones who stood in front of us, sang along, shouted requests, brought their friends, discovered new music, and made those nights worth repeating.

The ones who tolerated the imperfections because they understood the intention.

The ones who went on to create music of their own.

And the ones who simply showed up because, for a few hours, they wanted to be somewhere that felt like home.

You were as much a part of those six years as we were.

And if you ever walked through those doors, there's a good chance you helped keep something alive without even realizing it.

The club, the gear, the songs, and the faces belong to a particular chapter of our lives.

But what those nights represented is still very much alive.

Every time a young musician decides to start a band despite having no money, no connections, and no idea how anything works, the same spirit is there.

Every time somebody chooses to create instead of waiting for permission, the same fire burns.

And every time a handful of people gather in a small room to make some glorious, unreasonable noise, the underground lives another day.

From September 2005 to August 2011, that was our Saturday night.

We didn't light every fire in the underground.

**But for six years, we made damn sure ours never went out.**

And from that fire, others lit their own.

That's something we'll carry with us for the rest of our lives.

**Thank you people!**
**SYRGJYN**

*Since 1999. Still burning.*
